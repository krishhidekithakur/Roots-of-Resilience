Warming

A dry year hits harder in a warmer climate

These dry years are real history, not projections — but they now land in a warmer climate. Hotter air raises evaporative demand, so the same dry spell can stress crops more. Resilience matters even where rainfall trends are uncertain.

Price-covered stress test

Diversification didn't win every year.

Ten dry years. Same question. Did diversification make losing less costly?

It reduced the average downside.

Across the 10 price-covered dry stress events, average farm-gate-value downside was 6.6% under an equal-probability single-crop benchmark and 3.1% for the equal-third diversified hectare.

Price coverage is limited to 10 events in Fiji and New Caledonia. Treat this economic result as exploratory.

Lower average downside is a risk principle — not a universal planting recipe.

The same mix does not hedge every island the same way.

More than yield

A garden can be statistically resilient and still fail its community

Yield is only one dimension of resilience. In Pacific communities, crop choices can also carry cultural, ceremonial, exchange, labour, taste and place-based significance that tonnes alone cannot capture. In places including Samoa and Fiji, taro also carries important ceremonial and exchange value.

Crop as more than yield — dignity in the harvest.

Methods & sources

Physical stress test

Crop production and harvested area come from FAOSTAT, 1961–2024. Rainfall and surface-temperature anomalies come from the Pacific Data Hub Climate Change Indicators dataset, SPC:DF_CLIMATE_CHANGE(1.0). For each of the six analysis countries, the selected stress sample is the lowest 20% of available rainfall-anomaly observations by count. With 46 rainfall observations from 1979–2024, this gives 10 selected dry years per country and 60 country-year stress events in total. Crop yields are detrended separately by country and crop using a log-linear time trend. The interactive hectare is a hypothetical land allocation. Expected and historical harvests weight each crop by its fitted tonnes-per-hectare yield and the user's land share. It is not a simple arithmetic average of crop percentage anomalies.

Interpretation

The stress test is descriptive, not causal. National crop statistics are not household-garden observations, and selected historical country-years are not forecasts of future farm outcomes. “Unusually dry” is the project's analytical definition — the lowest 20% of rainfall-anomaly observations by country — not an official severe-drought classification.

Producer-price extension

Producer-price data come from FAOSTAT Agricultural Producer Prices. The economic extension uses annual Producer Price (USD/tonne) values for cassava, taro and sweet potato. Complete same-year USD producer prices for all three crops were available for 10 selected stress events: Fiji 1998, 2003, 2004, 2006, 2015; New Caledonia 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019. For each strategy, expected and historical crop quantities are valued using that country-crop-year's observed producer price.

The worst physical diversified stress test in this sample is Fiji 1992. Complete three-crop producer prices are not available for that year, so it is kept in tonnes and is not assigned a dollar figure.

The resulting quantity is gross farm-gate value relative to the fitted harvest benchmark. It is not farmer profit, household income or retail food value. Observed same-year producer prices are used as valuation weights. This analysis does not estimate how drought itself changes market prices.

Downside definition

Economic downside is defined as the negative portion of the farm-gate-value outcome: downside = max(−value outcome %, 0).

Single-crop benchmark

The no-diversification benchmark is the equal-probability average downside from committing the full hectare to one of cassava, taro or sweet potato before the outcome is known. This is a hypothetical ex-ante risk benchmark. It is not an estimate of actual farmer monocropping behaviour.

Statistical support

Across the 10 price-covered stress events, the average single-crop benchmark downside is approximately 6.63%, compared with 3.10% for the equal-third diversified hectare — a 3.53 percentage-point difference, or approximately 53.3% proportional reduction in average downside. Supporting paired checks give an exact sign-flip p-value of about 0.0078 and a paired Wilcoxon p-value of about 0.0122. An event-level bootstrap gives an approximate 95% interval of 1.37–5.91 percentage points. These inferential checks are exploratory. The price-covered sample contains only 10 events clustered within Fiji and New Caledonia, so the effect size and observed event pattern should carry more weight than a Pacific-wide interpretation of the p-values.
