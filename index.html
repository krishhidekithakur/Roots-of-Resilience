Why warming raises the stakes →
Warming

A dry year hits harder in a warmer climate

These dry years are real history, not projections — but they now land in a warmer climate. Hotter air raises evaporative demand, so the same dry spell can stress crops more. Resilience matters even where rainfall trends are uncertain.

No safe crop

The number that matters

How often did all three roots fail together?

4 / 60 — Fiji 2006, New Caledonia 2014, Tonga 1980 and 1981. In the other 56 dry years, at least one root held at or above trend.

Mechanism

Bad years don’t always coincide

Diversification only buffers when crops don’t move in lockstep. Pick a country: bars left of center are negative correlations — when one is up, another tends to be down.

More than yield

A garden can be statistically resilient and still fail its community

Yield is one dimension. Culture, preference, labour and choice are ones this model cannot measure — taro alone carries ceremonial weight across the Pacific that tonnes will never capture.

Crop as more than yield — dignity in the harvest.

Methods & sources

Methods & sources

Crop production and harvested area: FAOSTAT 1961–2024. Rainfall and surface-temperature anomalies: Pacific Data Hub Climate Change Indicators, SPC:DF_CLIMATE_CHANGE(1.0). Dry years = lowest 20% of rainfall anomalies per country (1979–2024) → 60 country-year events across Fiji, New Caledonia, Niue, Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands and Tonga. Yields detrended by country and crop (log-linear). The interactive garden combines crop shares with fitted tonnes-per-hectare yields — harvest change is not a simple average of percentage anomalies. Descriptive, not causal: national statistics ≠ household gardens; historical stress tests ≠ forecasts.
